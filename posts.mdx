## The LinkedIn post formula

```text
Line 1  — the hook. Short. It's the only line
          people see before "see more".
Line 2  — white space. Literally an empty line.
Body    — 3 to 6 short paragraphs. One idea. A concrete
          situation beats an abstraction every time.
Turn    — the reframe: what it looked like vs what it was.
Close   — a question, or a single quiet sentence. Not a CTA
          on most posts.
```

**Rhythm:** two posts a week. One diagnosis post (level 2), one that shows your way of seeing (level 1). Once a month, one offer post (level 3).

<Warning>
  **Never level 4 on LinkedIn.** See [the four levels](/messaging).
</Warning>

## Eight ready posts

Edit freely — these are shaped to be usable as-is. Each block has a copy button.

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Post 1 — diagnosis" icon="stethoscope">
    ```text
    The problem is almost never the person. It's the place.
    
    People usually call me about a team conflict. Two people who don't get along, an atmosphere that's gone heavy, a leader tired of refereeing.
    
    And almost every time, when we look closely, there is no conflict between people. There is someone occupying a place that isn't theirs — sometimes for years, sometimes because the company grew around them without anyone noticing.
    
    The tension isn't the problem. It's the information.
    
    Where is it rubbing, in your organisation, right now?
    ```
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Post 2 — the way of seeing" icon="eye">
    ```text
    In a healthy ecosystem, nothing is surplus.
    
    Every element has a function, a place, a relationship to the others. Remove one, move one, and everything else reorganises around it — sometimes well, often badly.
    
    A company works exactly the same way. Except we keep treating it like a machine: replace a part, add a process, redraw the org chart.
    
    And then we're surprised it doesn't hold.
    ```
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Post 3 — the leader's fatigue" icon="battery-low">
    ```text
    A leader's exhaustion is rarely a question of scheduling.
    
    I've met leaders working sixty-hour weeks who were completely fine. And others doing forty who couldn't take another day.
    
    The difference isn't the load. It's whether what you're carrying is actually yours to carry — or whether you're holding something that should have been set down somewhere else, a long time ago.
    
    What you're carrying right now: is it really yours?
    ```
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Post 4 — the reversal, softened into story" icon="rotate-ccw">
    Level 3 adjacent. This is [the credibility answer](/objections) told as a story rather than a claim.

    ```text
    For fifteen years, I didn't work with companies.
    
    I worked on one question: how human beings actually function. Not the theory — the observation, every day, for fifteen years.
    
    Then leaders started coming to me. Not for spirituality. For very concrete problems: a team coming apart, growth that was costing too much humanly, a vision they no longer recognised.
    
    And I realised I was looking at their company the way I look at everything else. As a living system.
    
    Most people who advise leaders made the opposite journey: they led first, then went looking for meaning. I'm not saying that's worse. I'm saying it isn't the same thing.
    ```
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Post 5 — growth" icon="trending-up">
    ```text
    Growing without losing your soul isn't founder naïveté.
    
    It's a question of structure.
    
    A company loses its soul when it grows faster than its ability to keep everyone in their right place. This has nothing to do with values printed on a wall. It has to do with who does what, and why.
    
    Values don't get lost. They drift.
    ```
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Post 6 — complexity" icon="network">
    ```text
    Complexity is not the price of success.
    
    We accept it as inevitable: the bigger you get, the more complicated it gets. More meetings, more process, more layers.
    
    But look at nature. A mature ecosystem isn't more complicated. It's more accurate. Everything in it is in its right place, and that is precisely what makes it simple to live in.
    
    If your company is getting complicated as it grows, that's not growth. That's a drift nobody has named yet.
    ```
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Post 7 — Era of Women" icon="venus">
    ```text
    I'm not asking anyone to make room for us.
    
    That phrasing has bothered me for a long time. To ask for a place is to accept that someone else hands it out.
    
    What I see in the women building things today is something else: they take their place, and once they're standing in it, they write different rules. Not out of opposition. Because the old ones simply didn't fit the way they work.
    
    That's far more interesting than a fight.
    ```
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Post 8 — offer post, once a month" icon="briefcase">
    ```text
    When a company is struggling, we almost always look in the wrong place.
    
    We look at process, at tools, at people. Rarely at the complete human system: who is in which place, and what that produces.
    
    That's what I do. A diagnostic of your company's human system — what's in its right place, what isn't, and what that's actually costing you.
    
    If you've felt for a while that something is off without being able to name it, this is probably the right moment. Write to me.
    ```
  </Accordion>

## Clip captions

For the short clips cut from each episode.

**Formula:** the strongest sentence from the clip, one line of context, where to find the full thing.

```text
"[strongest line from the clip]"

From this week's episode, on [topic].

Full episode: [link]
```

Post the same clip to **Instagram, Facebook and LinkedIn**. Different audiences, same asset. Your video editor handles the cutting — three to four clips per episode, spread across the following two weeks so one episode shows up repeatedly rather than once.
